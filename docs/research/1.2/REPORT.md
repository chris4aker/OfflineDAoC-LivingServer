# Build bots that remember, reach out, and fail

Written 2026-11-01. The research supports one central change for Living Server 1.2: **make the bots' social behaviour more reciprocal and their skill more fallible, and tie both to dated DAoC evidence rather than to genre canon or the model's own taste.** The six research strands agree that "alive" comes from being noticed, remembered and invited, and that bots read as fake when they react in unison, write tidy prose, or make mistakes no personality explains. The DAoC-sources strand settles the most consequential factual question. **No /lfg or /trade chat channel existed by patch 1.65 (8 October 2003).** Looking for a group was a flag-and-window system, and trade adverts were `/broadcast` macros, so the bots' current /lfg and /trade posts are an era error and should move. The bias audit shows that the project's quality numbers are too optimistic. The chat scored **95.6% on test sets its own authors wrote and 53.0% on a blind set**, and even the blind set is AI-written lines judged by AI against an AI-written "human" baseline. Several "era facts" also rest on European forum posters who were optimising their builds. The plan changes below therefore move work toward initiative and memory in chat, period-correct channels, competence written as a distribution, a world that does not wait for the player, and an evaluation anchored to real 2001–2003 text. Hard rules stay only for mistakes that would feel unfair to the human.

## Party AI: copy EverQuest's roles and stances, then add mistakes on purpose

Six shipped companion systems are worth studying, and they share a structure. At the bottom sits a fixed, hand-written brain for each role. Players get simple stances or presets on top of that, and enthusiasts get an ordered condition→action list above those. **EverQuest's mercenaries** fit DAoC best. Each archetype has named stances: a healer can be Balanced, Reactive, Efficient (conserves resources) or Passive, and a caster can be Balanced, Burn, Burn AE or Passive. A puller role is assigned from the group window, and a tank stance assists the group's main assist ([Almar's Guides](https://www.almarsguides.com/EQ/General/Mercenaries.cfm)). **Pillars of Eternity II** separates *when* a character engages (four autonomy levels, from Passive to Aggressive) from *what* it does (an ordered behaviour editor seeded with Aggressive and Cautious presets) ([Gamepressure](https://www.gamepressure.com/pillars-of-eternity-2/partys-ai/z1ae65)). That split matches two different Living Server complaints: bots that aggro too eagerly, and bots that use the wrong ability. **Dragon Age: Origins** unlocks tactic slots gradually, starting with 2 and adding more at set levels and through a skill line ([Dragon Age Wiki](https://dragonage.fandom.com/wiki/Combat_Tactics)). That avoids the blank-page problem of **FFXII's gambits**, whose designers built them so players could "manage the party without micromanaging" ([Game Developer, 2017](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/why-i-final-fantasy-iv-i-was-key-to-i-ffxii-i-s-ai-driven-gambit-system)). **WoW's follower dungeons** add two cheap ideas. They swap damage-dealer NPCs so none duplicates the player's class, and a single "Dungeon Assistance" toggle decides whether the bots lead or follow ([PC Gamer, 2024](https://www.pcgamer.com/world-of-warcraft-wow-follower-dungeons/)). **Valve's Left 4 Dead** bots contribute the fairness rules. Bots perceive only through simulated senses (field of view, line of sight, reaction time), make reliable decisions and never do the unforgivable thing such as friendly fire. They follow paths by steering toward a look-ahead point, because optimised paths "look robotic". Every state change logs a reason string for debugging ([Valve, 2009](https://cdn.fastly.steamstatic.com/apps/valve/2009/ai_systems_of_l4d_mike_booth.pdf)).

These mechanisms answer the four r/daoc complaints directly. Simulated senses address detection from too far away, which the 1.2 perception work already moves toward. Composition templates with class de-duplication and a cap on pet classes address the pet floods. Look-ahead steering with an offset per bot addresses every bot taking the same path. Stances, then group presets, then a choice of spec per bot address the missing custom specs. The mapping is a design inference, not a finding, and it carries two caveats. First, the survey covered only systems that succeeded and were documented. Guild Wars 1's heroes, probably the closest analogue to "customise my group's bots", could not be fetched and remain unverified. Mass Effect and Baldur's Gate 3 were not covered. Second, **every verified source is PvE**. No RvR or PvP companion-AI source was found, so applying these mechanisms to frontier warbands is an untested extrapolation.

The strand's strongest point is also where it was most biased. Crowd control is the documented weak spot even in Blizzard's version: WoW followers' **interrupts are unreliable** ([PC Gamer, 2024](https://www.pcgamer.com/world-of-warcraft-wow-follower-dungeons/)). The first draft concluded that "never break mez" must be a hard rule. The bias audit pushes back, and its case is stronger. Square Enix made Trust NPCs **deliberately less efficient than a skilled human group** ([GameSpot, 2019](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/ffxiv-shadowbringers-ai-companions-matter-more-tha/1100-6468377/)). Tarn Adams describes Dwarf Fortress dwarves being "allowed to make bigger mistakes", and players accept those mistakes because they fit the character: "I know what's going on" ([PC Gamer, 2025](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/sim/dwarf-fortress-dwarves-are-more-human-than-human-creator-says-theyre-allowed-to-embody-bigger-emotions-theyre-allowed-to-make-bigger-mistakes-theyre-allowed-to-do-anything)). Real 2003 groups broke mez, wiped on adds and ran gimped specs, and "who broke the mez" is itself a period joke. The right design is a **discipline trait for each bot** (mez discipline, spec quality, positioning, reaction) drawn from a distribution, with mistakes that follow the bot's personality. The group-wide "mezzed set" still exists so that a *disciplined* bot can respect it. Hard "never" rules should apply only to errors that would feel unfair to the human, such as a companion bot pulling a train onto the player's camp. Erenshor shows the opposite failure: its main complaint about SimPlayers is bots asking for gear **downgrades** ([Vaporlens](https://vaporlens.app/app/2382520/erenshor.md)). An error with no personality behind it reads as a bug.

## The channel question is settled: LFG was a window, trade was a broadcast macro

The bias audit rated the /lfg and /trade question as high severity and unresolved. The interim synthesis had built a channel list from a present-day wiki page that also described post-2004 features. The DAoC-sources strand closes the question with dated primary text. The answer is **no**: neither channel existed in the era.

|---|---|---|

| 23 Jan 2003 | Patch 1.59 | "The Looking for group system should now work across zones", storing up to 30 group leaders or solo players of each type ([1.59](https://camelotherald.fandom.com/wiki/Patch_Notes:_Version_1.59)) |

| spring–summer 2003 | Patch 1.62 | Chat tabs can send as "Say, Broadcast, Group, Guild, Alliance Chat, Officer Chat, or Chat Group", with no LFG or Trade type. Newbie guilds go live, four per realm ([1.62](https://camelotherald.fandom.com/wiki/Patch_Notes:_Version_1.62)) |

Five independent dated sources agree, and no source shows the opposite, so the question is closed. In 1.2, LFG behaviour should work like this: bots **set the LFG flag** so they appear in the player's LFG window, post short "LFG 24 ranger" lines in /say or /broadcast near towns and bind points, and recruit across zones through guild chat, public chat groups and, from 1.62, newbie-guild chat. Trade adverts should be /broadcast macros in towns. The Prima glossary defines broadcast as reaching "all in your town or city". 1.53 removed a duplicate broadcast region in Huginfell ([1.53](https://camelotherald.fandom.com/wiki/Patch_Notes:_Version_1.53)), which confirms that broadcast was area-scoped. There should be no zone-wide or server-wide trade channel. Keeping a convenience /lfg channel is a legitimate owner choice, but it should be labelled as a deviation in the settings and docs, not described as period behaviour.

The same evidence corrects two smaller items in the interim channel list. The **200-member chat group is dated after all**: it arrives in 1.60. That fits the project's own note of a 50-member cap at 1.50 and makes the cap date-dependent rather than wrong. **Battlegroup chat (/bc) as its own channel arrives in 1.66, three weeks after the cut.** It is absent from Prima's command table, so bots should not use it unless earlier evidence turns up. Broadcast in New Frontiers keeps and towers, and the "region chat" in the undated Camelot Herald beginner's guide, should both be treated as later features until they are dated. The question still open is when the LFG window first appeared. It existed by 1.59, and searches of the 1.40–1.58 notes found nothing.

### Sources the project has been under-crediting

These sources were reachable on 2026-11-01. The **Prima guide on archive.org** has full OCR text, was written with a named panel of expert players, and Mythic holds the copyright ([archive.org](https://archive.org/details/Dark_Age_of_Camelot_Revised_and_Expanded_Prima_Official_eGuide)). It is the best era etiquette source found. It says nobody owns a spawn site and groups "need to take turns". The only mobs you "own" are those you are pulling, fighting or have mezzed. Asking strangers for buffs is impolite because buffs cost the caster power. Kill-stealing can get a player booted. /advice was a player-run help channel whose advisors needed 15 hours of play. It also frames DAoC grouping against EverQuest's mez-and-assist habits, which is direct period evidence that players compared the game with EQ. The **fandom wiki's MediaWiki API** serves dated Herald patch notes even though its pages return 403. Those notes form a changelog of social features: /advice popups in 1.33 (October 2001), newbie guilds in 1.62, /rpfilter in 1.64 ([1.33](https://camelotherald.fandom.com/wiki/Patch_Notes:_Version_1.33)). **Matt Firor's February 2002 postmortem** shows that the Herald only began about six weeks after launch, so the first weeks of server culture ran on the Vault boards and in-game /advice ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/disciplines/postmortem-mythic-entertainment-s-i-dark-age-of-camelot-i-)). The **MUD-Dev archive** preserves era posts by Mythic designer Dave Rickey. In August 2002 he wrote that "even the wall between realms in Camelot leaks like a sieve" and that social networks "take the structure appropriate to their environment" ([MUD-Dev](https://mud-dev.zer7.com/2002/8/24813/)). **GameBanshee's news archive** reposts Herald Grab Bag columns with dates ([GameBanshee](https://www.gamebanshee.com/news/69188-camelot-grab-bag-qa-v15-69188.html)). The **alt.games.dark-age-of-camelot** Usenet group can be browsed with dates back to 2001 ([Google Groups](https://groups.google.com/g/alt.games.dark-age-of-camelot)). The **Wayback Machine** still resets the connection, but its availability API works and confirms that a June 2002 Vault boards snapshot exists ([archive.org API](http://archive.org/wayback/available?url=vnboards.ign.com&timestamp=20020601)). The US-side evidence therefore exists. It needs a session that can reach web.archive.org.

One conflict between strands should be stated. The DAoC-sources strand found Usenet "buffbot" threads only from 2004–2005 and concluded that buffbots should not be assumed for 2003. But the dated September 2002 FreddysHouse thread already uses "buff-bots" as slang ([FreddysHouse](https://forums.freddyshouse.com/threads/return-to-camelot.98800)). The term and the complaint were current by late 2002, at least on European servers. How common buffbots were is unknown.

## Chat and realm speak: short, social, uneven, and sometimes wrong

The quantitative literature points in a useful direction but cannot supply targets. Peña and Hancock found recreational game chat to be **about 77% socioemotional and 23% task**, with positive social talk outnumbering negative almost 3 to 1. Standard deviations were larger than the means, so a few people talked a lot and most barely spoke. Experienced players used conventions (emotes, abbreviations, emoticons) in about half their messages ([Peña & Hancock, 2006](https://socialmedialab.sites.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj22976/files/media/file/pena-cr-an-analysis.pdf)). The bias audit is right that this is **65 people on a Jedi Knight II clan server**, a shooter, and not a DAoC camp or keep take. Treat it as a direction: more social than task talk, heavily skewed per speaker, more conventions with experience. Do not make "3 social to 1 task" a tuning target. Iorio's City of Heroes work adds the most transferable finding: spelling variation follows how public the message is, the player's cognitive load and their social group, so style carries meaning rather than being noise ([Iorio, 2010](https://tdl-ir.tdl.org/handle/2152/ETD-UT-2010-05-727)). That supports **per-bot idiolects** (favourite tokens, typo habits, a chosen emoticon) and a register that tightens in public channels and loosens in guild chat.

The DAoC-specific culture evidence is thin, and much of it comes from one place. The realm stereotypes ("the greenpeace of DAoC" for Hibernia, Midgard "elitists", "avoid Albion on Excalibur like the plague") and the lowercase, typo-heavy style come from **one** September 2002 FreddysHouse thread about two European servers ([FreddysHouse](https://forums.freddyshouse.com/threads/return-to-camelot.98800)). They are good flavour. They are not evidence of US norms. The Prima guide adds usable etiquette: calling targets and power levels in group, "Mez invaders first!" for bards, and the glossary's ks, kos, ld, baf, brb and cfh ([archive.org](https://archive.org/details/Dark_Age_of_Camelot_Revised_and_Expanded_Prima_Official_eGuide)). It has no hits for "zerg", so the period syntax of RvR calls ("inc 5 from W") is still unsourced.

The living-world strand explains why the bots read as fake. In Old School RuneScape, one player typed "How Many" and hundreds of bots answered with strings of numbers ([OpenCritic, 2025](https://opencritic.com/news/20960/runescape-player-discovers-hilariously-effective-way-to-uncover-bots)). Uniform mass reactions are the fastest giveaway. The opposite signal is initiative. Erenshor's developer says his SimPlayers, built from decision trees and pre-written lines with no language model, "remember if you wiped or if you had a great group", greet you by name and eventually whisper invites when you log in ([developer post, 2024](https://redlib.hyperreal.coffee/r/MMORPG/comments/1g8u0q1/erenshor_a_simulated_mmorpg)). Players' praise centres on personalities, guild chat and whispers ([Vaporlens](https://vaporlens.app/app/2382520/erenshor.md)). The audit's warning applies here. This is one well-received indie game with a small cast **tethered to the player's level**, and the evidence is one developer post, one thread and a review aggregator. Reciprocity (greetings, memory, invites) probably transfers to thousands of untethered bots. "Your friends progress with you" probably only works for a small social circle. The mod-ollama-chat project for WoW playerbots adds a brake that works for any chat system: an **audience rule**, under which bots reply to bots only if a real player has spoken recently in that channel, together with decaying odds of replying in bot-to-bot chains ([GitHub](https://github.com/DustinHendrickson/mod-ollama-chat)).

The audit also identifies two biases built into the current chat goals. The first is **accuracy**. The project's research says to avoid "perfectly accurate advice". But the blind evaluation penalises "beats me" and plans to route place questions to a knowledge lookup (`eval/chat-blind-2026-11-01:docs/bot-voices/CHAT-EVAL.md`). Period /advice was full of half-right answers. Bots should get an **error budget by experience tier**: newbies wrong or vague, regulars mostly right, veterans confident and sometimes out of date. Replies should be graded on plausibility, not correctness. The second is **purity rules mixed with taste**. The writing guide bans EverQuest by name, yet the Prima guide itself frames DAoC grouping against EQ. The guide bans profanity, while the brief admits period chat "was saltier". It also allows xX and number names that the 2003 name filter could not produce (`release/1.2:docs/bot-voices/WRITING-GUIDE.md`, `release/1.2:docs/research/BOT-NAMES-2003.md`). Each of these is a defensible owner choice. The fault is that the docs then call the chat "period-accurate". Every rule should be tagged "evidence" or "owner choice", and generic "eq" comparisons should come back.

## Dialogue and world design: salience rules, a social-facts log, and a world that moves without you

For the dialogue engine the evidence still favours the Left 4 Dead family. Valve's system matches lines against a changing world state, applies "the most specific one it can find", falls back to generic lines, and lets characters break off an exchange when one is attacked ([Emily Short, 2012](https://emshort.blog/2012/03/16/gdc-2012-talk-on-dynamic-dialogue/)). Salience systems let writers start broad and add specific lines without breaking old ones. They also pace badly without state that tracks the arc, and tuning them is "largely trial and error" ([Emily Short, 2016](https://emshort.blog/2016/04/12/beyond-branching-quality-based-and-salience-based-narrative-structures/)). Naughty Dog's Last of Us system splits what each character knows from what the group knows ([GDC Vault, 2014](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1020386/A-Context-Aware-Character-Dialog)), which maps directly onto a group blackboard and a blackboard per bot. Watch Dogs: Legion scaled to unlimited speakers with **20 writing personas**, doubled from 10 to cover dialects. Writers could not reference a recruit's individual traits, and deliberately "weird" generated bios kept characters memorable ([Game Developer, 2023](https://gamedeveloper.com/gdc2023/how-i-watch-dogs-legion-i-made-ubisoft-rethink-its-storytelling)). For Living Server that suggests personas that fix *what* each type talks about and *how often*, not only its spelling. Valve's response flags (`odds`, `norepeat`, expiring contexts) are the right toolkit, but their exact semantics were not verified this round because the Valve wiki was blocked. Build them as the project's own design, not as a copy of Valve's.

Memory needs a structure that dialogue can query. Comme il Faut, the engine behind Prom Week, keeps a **Social Facts Database** of labelled past events that both choice rules and dialogue templates can name. It needed **over 5,000 influence rules**, and contradictory rules appeared ([McCoy et al., 2014](https://cs.uky.edu/~sgware/reading/papers/mccoy2014cif.pdf)). A light version (actor, target, label, time, plus an affinity score) is cheap. It turns "the bot remembers you" into lines like "that wipe at the barrows". The rule count needs a budget from the start. A Nemesis-style rival (enemy-realm bots who killed you or were killed by you, and come back remembering it) fits RvR. Monolith's lesson is that rivalry needs the player to lose sometimes ([Game Developer, 2017](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/upgrading-the-nemesis-system-for-i-middle-earth-shadow-of-war-i-)). Warner Bros. holds a patent on the Nemesis system, so the hierarchy and promotion mechanics should not be copied closely.

The audit's sharpest design finding is that **the world is too centred on the player while the social circle is not centred enough**. The live-events plan starts major events only while the human is logged in, never resolves them to failure offline, saves one decisive beat for the player and drops notices when no player of the realm is online (`release/1.2:docs/LIVE-EVENTS-ENGINE.md` §4, §6). A 2003 server's defining trait was that it went on without you. Your keep fell overnight. S.T.A.L.K.E.R.'s A-Life shows the mechanism: full simulation near the player, abstract resolution on a zone graph elsewhere, with the results applied over game time ([Game Developer, 2017](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/a-life-an-insight-into-ambitious-ai)). It also shows the risk. A-Life 2.0 shipped broken in 2024 ([PC Gamer](https://pcgamer.com/games/fps/stalker-2-devs-reassure-players-yes-a-life-2-0-is-in-the-game-no-its-not-working-right-and-yes-fixes-are-on-the-way)), and Ultima Online cut its ecology for performance ([Koster](https://www.raphkoster.com/?p=46439)). The cheap layer has to stay consistent with what the player later sees. So the two fixes point in opposite directions. Let some majors resolve offline and report them at login through bot gossip ("you missed it"). Point the initiative at the player's own circle.

Two earlier conclusions need tempering. **RimWorld's storyteller** (state read before each event, an adaptation score, pacing personalities) ([RimWorld Wiki](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Storyteller)) was chosen partly because it is well documented. That is not the same as fitting a server of 3,000 bots. Use its ideas, such as breathing room after a hard event and pressure that eases after the player suffers. Do not treat it as the architecture, and vary outcomes, including honest routs, rather than scripting near-wipes toward a 60–75% success rate taken from a single quote. **Small local language models** remain out of scope for the base system. The shipped uses are narrow (inZOI's roughly 0.5B model on RTX cards only, behind an experimental toggle, driving behaviour rather than chat) ([NVIDIA, 2025](https://www.nvidia.com/en-gb/geforce/news/nvidia-ace-naraka-bladepoint-inzoi-launch-this-month/)). The one benchmark found ran 0.8–35 s per reply on an RTX 2070 Super ([arXiv 2511.10277](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.10277v1)). Ultima Online's collapsed resource pool is the other borrowed lesson: with real loot, coins and the Realm Exchange, the bot economy needs explicit faucets and drains ([Koster](https://www.raphkoster.com/?p=46439)).

## The evaluation measures the model's taste, not 2003's

The project's own numbers set the agenda. Test sets written by the chat's authors read as expected 95.6% and 95.9% of the time, while a blind set read as expected 53.0% (`eval/chat-blind-2026-11-01:docs/bot-voices/CHAT-EVAL.md`). The blind round is a real improvement, but it inherits three distortions. Language-model judges favour their own generations, and how well a model recognises its own text tracks how much it prefers it ([Panickssery et al., 2024](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/7f1f0218e45f5414c79c0679633e47bc-Abstract-Conference.html)). The "player" lines in spot-the-bot were model-written, so the test asked whether bot lines stand out from period-*style* lines, not from real ones. And the blind misses cluster in the tails: roleplay (16 of 17 missed), banter, offers of help and RvR calls. AI-written baselines under-represent exactly those. The 72% catch rate is probably a lower bound on how bot-like the lines are against real logs, and the reply-quality scores are probably upper bounds. Humans are no automatic fix. People could not detect AI self-presentations across 4,600 participants, because they relied on flawed cues ([Jakesch et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.07271v4)). In the 2012 BotPrize, two bots scored **52% humanness against the human players' 40%** ([Gizmodo, 2012](https://gizmodo.com/how-did-this-game-bot-score-higher-than-humans-on-a-tur-5947796)). A human panel needs calibration items and an anchor of real text. The interim report's line that the blind test is "already more measurement than most" studios do should be dropped. No studio repetition metrics were found, which is an absence of evidence rather than a lead.

The in-game evidence is narrow as well. The owner plays about five hours a week. The logged checks are mostly one Albion Cleric, sometimes a GM, with 50 to 300 bots a realm, against a design target of 600–1,000 bots a realm (`release/1.2:docs/RELEASE-TRAIN.md`, `release/1.2:docs/LIVING-SERVER.md`). Some sessions were run by an AI agent reading the logs. Those show that systems fired, not how the game felt, and AGENTS.md already requires them to be reported separately from human play. The data also carries a source skew. The build weights come from 2,666 European build posts in threads with titles like "the perfect albion rvr group". The doc says no US spec source was cited. Some weights invert their own post counts: Cleric Smite has **0** dated posts and a weight of 0.20 (`feature/era-builds:docs/research/ERA-BUILDS-1.65.md`). Writing the forum's "perfect 8" into every bot group makes the population too competent and too organised. That is the same competence bias as the hard mez rule, reached from the data side.

## What should change in the 1.2 plan

The table ranks changes by value for cost. "Evidence" means a dated source supports the change. "Bias fix" means it corrects a skew the audit found. "Design" means it is a borrowed mechanism whose fit is inferred.

|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Move LFG and trade talk to period channels.** LFG goes to the window flag plus /say and /broadcast in towns, guild and chat groups. Trade goes to /broadcast macros. No /bc before 1.66. Keep any /lfg channel only as a labelled owner deviation | Evidence (Prima 2002; 1.59–1.65) | Re-route the bot chat emitters. Make bots set the LFG flag so they fill the player's LFG window |
| 2 | **Gate social features by patch date**: chat-group cap (200 from 1.60), newbie guilds with their own chat (1.62), /advice advisors, /rpfilter (1.64) | Evidence (Herald notes) | Add a "source date" field to every era fact. Add newbie-guild membership for low-level bots, who leave on joining a real guild |
| 3 | **Anchor evaluation to real period text.** 300–500 verbatim dated lines used only as the human side of spot-the-bot and as a style anchor, never as line text. Judges from a different model family, with the model recorded for each role. Own-set scores kept as regression tests only | Bias fix | Mine the Prima guide, FreddysHouse "funny chat log" threads, Usenet and the Konstanz logs. ### Research and tests still to do

Four research items stand out. First, reach web.archive.org from a session where it works, for the US side: Vault class boards (a June 2002 snapshot is confirmed to exist), Camelot Seer and Herald census pages. Then compare US class shares with the European-derived weights and record the difference before freezing `bot-builds.json`. Second, establish when the LFG window first appeared (before 1.59) and whether battlegroup chat existed in any form before 1.66. Third, verify the Guild Wars 1 hero controls, the closest unverified analogue for customising group bots, and find at least one *failed* example of each borrowed pattern, to offset the survey's focus on successes. Fourth, extract the bodies of Rickey's MUD-Dev "Grouping in MMP Games" and "Ten Rules of PvP" posts, which the page parse missed, and look for period RvR call syntax and RP-server norms, which no source has documented yet.

The tests should change as much as the research. Build a matrix that covers all three realms; a healer, a stealther, a pet class and a melee class; both non-GM and GM; full rosters of 600 or more per realm; one level-1 newcomer session; and at least one outside player each release. Tag each session "human-played" or "agent-run", and check whether GM status changes how bots treat the player. Run a population-level quiet target (lines per channel per hour by time of day, including a "dead hour" baseline) in the planned 900-bot hour, plus a silent-night A/B session for the owner. Give the r/daoc panel calibration items (known-real and known-bot lines) and weight judges by how accurately they call those. Rate "felt like 2003" separately from "fun now". Every simulator target, including the live-events rehearsal transcript, needs a matching in-game check and a few honest "how it felt" notes before it counts as done.

## Conclusion

The research changes what kind of problem 1.2 is solving. The bots do not mainly need more skill or more lines. They need to look less coordinated and less polished: fewer of them reacting, more of them occasionally wrong, groups that sometimes fail for reasons a player can recognise, and a world whose events do not depend on the human being online. The same smoothing shows up in four places: the build data (forum optimisers), the chat evaluation (one model family's idea of a player), the group design (hard competence rules) and the events engine (outcomes arranged around the player). The common fix is to add back the variance the sources and tools removed.

The channel finding also changes how the project should treat era claims. Five dated sources settled in one session a question that a later wiki page had left wrong, and the patch notes turn out to be a dated changelog of social features. That makes dates checkable: any era claim without a source date should be treated as a lead to verify, and feature flags can follow the patch timeline. The European-derived build weights are the obvious next claim to check against US sources.
